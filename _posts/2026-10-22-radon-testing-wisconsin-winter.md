Radon is a radioactive gas that comes naturally from rocks, soil and water. You can't see it or smell it. According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html), it can get into homes through small cracks and holes and build up indoors, whether a house is new or old, drafty or tight, with or without a basement.

As Wisconsin heads into heating season, a common question is whether now is a good time to test. Short answer: yes. Here is why, and how to do it right.

## Why radon is worth a test

Radon is a slow, long-term risk, not an emergency. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon) calls it the second leading cause of lung cancer overall and the leading cause among people who have never smoked. The agency estimates about 21,000 U.S. lung cancer deaths a year from radon, with an uncertainty range of 8,000 to 45,000. About 2,900 of those deaths are in people who never smoked.

Smoking makes radon much more dangerous. In EPA's risk table, at 4 pCi/L (picocuries per liter) about 62 in 1,000 smokers could get lung cancer over a lifetime, compared with about 7 in 1,000 people who never smoked. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html) adds that there is no known safe level of radon. It also notes there isn't enough data to say whether children face a higher lung cancer risk than adults, though they may breathe in higher doses.

Globally, the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health) estimates that radon causes 3% to 14% of lung cancers in a country, depending on average radon levels and how common smoking is.

## How common is high radon in Wisconsin?

The [Wisconsin Department of Health Services (DHS)](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/news/releases/010625.htm) says about 1 in 10 Wisconsin homes has elevated radon. It also estimates that roughly 962 Wisconsinites have radon-induced lung cancer. You can't tell which homes are affected by looking at them or by checking a neighbor's result. The [DHS radon program](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/index.htm) says testing is the only way to know and recommends that every home be tested.

## Why winter testing makes sense

The [Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/news/releases/010625.htm) notes that indoor radon levels tend to rise in the colder months. Its [testing guidance](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/radon-results.htm) says you can test any time of year, but winter is an especially good time because it's easier to keep "closed building conditions," meaning windows and doors stay shut.

That matters because short-term tests depend on closed conditions. EPA's [home buyer's and seller's guide](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=10004BMM.txt) defines closed-house conditions as keeping windows closed, keeping doors closed except for normal entry and exit, and not running fans or other machines that bring in outside air.

One honest caveat: a single winter test is a snapshot, not your yearly average. DHS says long-term tests (at least 91 days, often 6 to 12 months, covering both heating and non-heating seasons) are the best way to estimate your annual exposure.

## Practical steps: running a short-term test

These steps follow the [Wisconsin DHS testing page](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/radon-results.htm) unless noted.

1. **Get a kit.** DHS says kits cost about $10 to $30 at hardware stores, online, or through a regional Radon Information Center. Some centers offer free or reduced-cost kits.
2. **Close up the house.** Keep exterior doors and windows closed for at least 12 hours before the test starts and throughout the test. Normal coming and going is fine. Run your heating as usual.
3. **Pick the right spot.** Use the lowest level where someone spends at least seven hours a week. For real estate deals, test the lowest level, such as the basement.
4. **Place it correctly.** Set the kit 2 to 7 feet above the floor and at least 3 feet from exterior doors, windows and air vents. Avoid bathrooms, kitchens, crawlspaces and direct sunlight.
5. **Follow the kit's timing.** Short-term tests usually run two to four days. Then send it to the lab as the kit instructions direct.

## What to do with your result

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon) action level is 4 pCi/L. The [Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/radon-results.htm) describes the next steps:

- **Below 4 pCi/L:** No mitigation needed. Retest every 2 to 5 years, and after major work on the foundation or heating and cooling system, or after weatherization such as new windows and doors.
- **4 to 7 pCi/L:** Do a second test, either long-term or short-term. If the average of the two results is above 4 pCi/L, DHS recommends mitigation.
- **8 pCi/L and above:** Do a second short-term test right away. If the average is above 4 pCi/L, DHS recommends hiring a radon contractor.

## How radon is fixed

EPA's [Consumer's Guide to Radon Reduction](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_consumers_guide_to_radon_reduction.pdf) says active subslab suction, also called subslab depressurization, is the most common and usually the most reliable method. Some systems can lower radon by up to 99%. A few other points from the guide:

- EPA does not recommend sealing cracks by itself, because sealing alone hasn't been shown to lower radon significantly or consistently.
- The system fan should never be turned off. It has to run all the time to work.
- Retest within 30 days of installation, but no sooner than 24 hours after the system starts running. EPA suggests an independent test or doing it yourself.
- EPA recommends a certified or qualified radon mitigation contractor, and suggests checking with your state radon office first.

If you have a private well, the same guide suggests considering testing both air and water. Radon from soil is usually the much bigger concern.

## Where HVAC fits in

Radon mitigation is its own specialty. Duct cleaning and air filters are not radon fixes. The method EPA describes pulls soil gas from under the slab and vents it outside. Your heating system still matters to the test, though: DHS asks you to run it normally during the test and to retest after major changes to heating or cooling. If a [duct or HVAC professional](https://ductsoncall.com) is working on your system this winter, that is a good reminder to put a radon test on your list too.

Radon isn't the only winter indoor air concern. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html) lists burning wood, coal or other materials that add particles to the air as a factor that raises lung cancer risk from radon. For more on particles, see our post on [PM2.5 indoors](/2026/10/22/pm25-indoors-what-the-science-says/).

## Bottom line

About 1 in 10 Wisconsin homes has elevated radon, and the only way to know about yours is to test. Winter is a practical time to run a short-term test because the house is already closed up. Follow the kit instructions, confirm any result of 4 pCi/L or higher with a second test, and if mitigation is needed, use a qualified radon contractor and retest afterward.
