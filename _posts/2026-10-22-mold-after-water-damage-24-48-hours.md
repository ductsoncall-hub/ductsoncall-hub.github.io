Mold spores are everywhere, indoors and out. Mold only grows when it has moisture. The [EPA's guide to mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) puts it simply: the key to controlling mold is controlling moisture.

## The 24–48 hour rule

The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. After that, porous materials such as drywall, carpet padding and ceiling tiles that stayed wet may need to be removed instead of cleaned.

## What the health evidence shows

The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) reports that damp, moldy environments can cause nasal stuffiness, wheezing, and eye or skin irritation. People with allergies, asthma or weakened immune systems are at higher risk. A major [Institute of Medicine review](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/11011/damp-indoor-spaces-and-health) found enough evidence to link damp indoor spaces with upper respiratory symptoms, cough, wheeze and asthma symptoms in sensitized people. The [WHO's guidelines on dampness and mould](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789289041683) reached similar conclusions.

## Humidity targets

The EPA recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60%, and ideally between 30% and 50%. An inexpensive hygrometer can measure it.

## Practical steps

- Fix leaks fast and dry wet materials within 24–48 hours.
- Vent bathrooms, kitchens and clothes dryers to the outdoors.
- Keep HVAC drip pans clean and drain lines flowing freely.
- Insulate cold surfaces, such as some ducts and pipes, where condensation forms.
- For areas larger than about 10 square feet, the EPA suggests consulting its [remediation guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-remediation-schools-and-commercial-buildings-guide) or hiring a professional.
