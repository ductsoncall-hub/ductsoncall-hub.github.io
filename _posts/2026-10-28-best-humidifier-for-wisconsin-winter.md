By January, a lot of Wisconsin homes feel dry: static shocks, cracked lips, a scratchy throat in the morning. A humidifier can help with comfort, but it is also the one appliance in this series that can make indoor air worse if you buy the wrong type or let it run unchecked. This guide covers the two things most "best humidifier" lists skip for our area, hard water and over-humidifying, and then the models independent testers rate highest.

## The ceiling matters more than the floor

The EPA's guidance on home humidifiers is direct: "Do not humidify to indoor relative humidity levels exceeding 50 percent," because higher humidity can encourage biological growth such as dust mites and mold ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers)). It also says that if water condenses on windows, walls or pictures, you should move the humidifier, lower its setting or use it less.

In a cold climate the safe winter number is lower still. The Minnesota Department of Health suggests 20% to 40% relative humidity in winter to keep surfaces dry ([Minnesota Department of Health](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/air/mold/index.html)). Our article on [indoor humidity in a Wisconsin winter](/2026/10/23/indoor-humidity-wisconsin-winter/) explains why the target drops as the outdoor temperature falls. The practical takeaway: buy a humidifier with a humidistat, or pair it with a separate hygrometer, and treat window condensation as a signal to turn it down.

## Why Madison's tap water changes the choice

Madison Water Utility describes its tap water as "very hard," at 18 to 20 grains of hardness per gallon, from the calcium and magnesium in the dolomite aquifer ([City of Madison](https://www.cityofmadison.com/water/water-quality/faq)). The Madison Metropolitan Sewerage District puts the area range at roughly 15 to 32 grains per gallon depending on the well ([MMSD](https://madsewer.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/ECO-2023-Softener-Training-Workbook.pdf)). Anything above 7 grains counts as hard ([City of Madison Waterways](https://www.cityofmadison.com/engineering/documents/MadisonWaterways2025.pdf)).

That matters because of how each humidifier type handles minerals. According to the EPA, ultrasonic and impeller ("cool mist") humidifiers "generally appear to produce the greatest dispersions of both microorganisms and minerals," while evaporative and steam units "are not expected to disperse substantial amounts of minerals" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers)). The minerals show up as "white dust" on furniture. HouseFresh measured fine particles while running humidifiers on tap water and recorded 879.4 µg/m³ of PM2.5 from one popular ultrasonic model ([HouseFresh](https://housefresh.com/the-best-humidifiers-we-tested/)). For context on what fine particles mean indoors, see [PM2.5 indoors: what the science says](/2026/10/22/pm25-indoors-what-the-science-says/).

You have two reasonable paths:

1. **Evaporative humidifier with tap water.** A fan blows air through a wet wick. Wirecutter notes the wick helps keep minerals out of the air, so you can fill it from the tap ([Wirecutter](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/the-best-humidifier/)). RTINGS adds that evaporative units are mechanically simpler but noisier because of the fan ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/humidifier/reviews/best/humidifiers)).
2. **Ultrasonic humidifier with distilled water.** Quieter, but Wirecutter says every ultrasonic model it recommends needs distilled or filtered water to avoid mineral residue ([Wirecutter](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/the-best-humidifier/)). The EPA calls distillation "the most effective method for removing minerals" and notes that bottled "spring" or "mineral" water has not been treated to remove them ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers)). In a Madison winter that can mean buying a gallon or more of distilled water every day or two.

If you won't haul distilled water all winter, buy evaporative.

## Sizing

RTINGS sizes humidifiers by tested output in liters per day (LPD): 2.0 to 3.5 LPD for 100 to 300 sq ft, 3.5 to 6.0 for 300 to 500 sq ft, 6.0 to 9.0 for 500 to 1,000 sq ft, and above 9.0 for more than 1,000 sq ft. It also warns that an oversized unit can keep humidity too high and contribute to mold ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/humidifier/reviews/best/humidifiers)). For most Wisconsin homes, a bedroom-sized or living-room-sized unit with a humidistat is the right starting point.

One cold-climate note from RTINGS: cool-mist units can slightly lower the room temperature as the water evaporates, which makes the furnace work a little harder ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/humidifier/reviews/best/humidifiers)).

## Keep it clean, or don't run it

The EPA's care routine for portable units: empty, wipe dry and refill the tank daily, and clean every third day, removing scale and film. If the manufacturer doesn't specify a cleaner, the EPA suggests 3% hydrogen peroxide, then several rinses ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers)). It also notes that microorganisms often grow in tanks with standing water, and advises stopping use and contacting a physician if you notice respiratory symptoms you associate with the humidifier.

At the end of the season, clean and fully dry the unit, discard used cartridges or filters, and store it somewhere dry.

## What about a furnace humidifier?

Whole-house humidifiers mount on the furnace and humidify through the ductwork. Consumer Reports does not test them ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/humidifiers/buying-guide/)), and the EPA says to follow the manufacturer's maintenance directions for central units ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers)). If you have one, check the water panel or pad each fall and set the control by outdoor temperature, as covered in our [winter humidity article](/2026/10/23/indoor-humidity-wisconsin-winter/).
