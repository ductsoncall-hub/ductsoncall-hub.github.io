Hardware store filter aisles show a wall of numbers: MERV 8, MERV 11, MERV 13, plus brand scales like FPR and MPR. Higher numbers cost more, so the natural question is whether they're worth it and whether your furnace can handle them. Here's what U.S. EPA guidance actually says.

## What a MERV rating measures

MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. According to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/what-merv-rating), it reports a filter's ability to capture particles between 0.3 and 10 microns.

The scale runs from 1 to 16. Lower ratings mostly catch larger particles. Higher ratings also catch the finest size range tested. A few points from EPA's table:

| MERV | Smallest particles (0.3–1.0 µm) | Mid-size (1.0–3.0 µm) | Larger (3.0–10 µm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | not rated | ≥20% | ≥70% |
| 11 | ≥20% | ≥65% | ≥85% |
| 13 | ≥50% | ≥85% | ≥90% |
| 16 | ≥95% | ≥95% | ≥95% |

The [EPA's Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) explains that MERV is based on a national consensus standard, while FPR and MPR are proprietary scales. For every scale, a higher rating means higher removal for most particle sizes. If you're comparing brands, MERV is the common yardstick.

The 0.3–1.0 micron range overlaps with the fine particles discussed in our post on [PM2.5 indoors](/2026/10/22/pm25-indoors-what-the-science-says/). That's why the jump from MERV 8 to MERV 13 matters more than it looks.

## Why EPA points to MERV 13

EPA's advice is consistent across its pages. If you upgrade, "choose a filter with at least a MERV 13 rating, or as high a rating as your system fan and filter slot can accommodate" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/what-merv-rating)). The [air cleaner guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) notes that MERV 13 and above filters must show at least 50% removal of the smallest particles tested.

## Will a higher-MERV filter hurt my furnace?

This is the most common worry, and EPA's answer is cautious. The [guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) says "most furnaces and HVAC systems can accommodate a MERV 13 filter without creating equipment problems, provided that the filter is replaced frequently." It also admits it can be hard to tell for sure, because HVAC systems usually aren't labeled with filter recommendations. To be certain, EPA suggests asking an HVAC technician. A technician can figure out the most efficient filter your system can use, or modify the system to fit a higher-efficiency filter.

Thickness matters too. EPA notes filters come in thicknesses of 1 to 5 inches. The filter should fit snugly so air doesn't leak around it, and you shouldn't have to bend or crush it to make it fit.

## The catch: filters only work when the fan runs

A furnace filter can be excellent on paper and still filter little air if the system rarely runs. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) points out that furnace and HVAC filters work only when the system is operating. In most cases, that's less than 25% of the time during heating and cooling seasons. Running the system longer filters more air. EPA also notes that longer run times raise electricity costs and can make humidity control less reliable in cooling season.

Filtration also isn't the first line of defense. EPA's [air cleaners and filters page](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/air-cleaners-and-air-filters-home) says the most effective ways to improve indoor air are to reduce or remove pollutant sources and to ventilate with clean outdoor air. It describes filtration as "an effective supplement." It also says filters "cannot remove all pollutants from the air."

## Furnace filters vs. HEPA portable air cleaners

HEPA stands for "high efficiency particulate air," a standard defined by the U.S. Department of Energy. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/what-hepa-filter) says a HEPA filter can theoretically remove at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns in size. HEPA filters are common in portable air cleaners.

Portable units are rated by clean air delivery rate (CADR). The [EPA guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) suggests choosing one with a CADR large enough for the room. Its estimates, which assume 8-foot ceilings, include:

| Room size | Minimum CADR |
|---|---|
| 200 sq ft | 130 cfm |
| 300 sq ft | 195 cfm |
| 400 sq ft | 260 cfm |

On health effects, EPA is careful. It says studies of portable HEPA air cleaners have shown small improvements in heart and breathing measures. Multiple studies found improvement in one or more allergy or asthma symptoms. But the improvements are often small, not always noticeable, and don't cover every symptom.

Two more cautions from the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home):

- **Particles and gases are different problems.** Most filters are designed to filter either particles or gases. To filter gases, EPA suggests a unit with activated carbon or another filter designed for gases, and there are no widely used performance ratings for gas filtration. CADR covers particles only.
- **Avoid ozone.** EPA advises avoiding air cleaners and filters that intentionally produce ozone, a lung irritant. It notes that no federal agency has approved ozone generators for use in occupied spaces.

## Practical steps

1. **Check what fits.** Note the size and thickness printed on your current filter, and make sure any replacement fits snugly without bending.
2. **Aim for MERV 13 if your system allows it.** If you're not sure, ask an HVAC technician ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)).
3. **Replace on schedule.** EPA says manufacturers typically recommend replacing filters every 60 to 90 days. If filters look heavily soiled when you change them, change them more often. A dirty, overloaded filter won't work well.
4. **Add a portable HEPA unit for a bedroom or main room.** Size it by CADR to the room.
5. **Fix sources first.** EPA ranks reducing pollutant sources and ventilating with clean outdoor air ahead of filtration ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/air-cleaners-and-air-filters-home)).

## Where ducts fit in

A filter can only clean air that passes through it. If a filter doesn't fit snugly, air can leak around it instead of through it, which is why EPA stresses a snug fit. If you're upgrading filters and want the system checked, a [duct and HVAC professional](https://ductsoncall.com) can look at filter fit and the condition of your ductwork.

## Bottom line

MERV measures how well a filter captures particles from 0.3 to 10 microns. EPA recommends at least MERV 13, or the highest rating your system can handle, and says most home systems can use MERV 13 if you change it often. A furnace filter only works while the fan runs. A properly sized HEPA portable cleaner can help in key rooms. Neither one replaces source control and ventilation.
