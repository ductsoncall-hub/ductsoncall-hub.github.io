When the furnace starts running for the winter, it's a good time to check your carbon monoxide (CO) alarms. You can't see or smell CO, so an alarm is the only practical way to know it's building up. This post covers what CO is, why the risk rises in cold months, and where official guidance says alarms belong.

## What carbon monoxide is and where it comes from

The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) describes CO as "an odorless, colorless gas" produced when fuel is burned. It is found in fumes from cars and trucks, small engines, stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces, gas ranges and furnaces.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/carbon-monoxides-impact-indoor-air-quality) lists common indoor sources:

- leaking chimneys and furnaces
- back-drafting from furnaces, gas water heaters, wood stoves and fireplaces
- unvented kerosene and gas space heaters
- gas stoves
- generators
- car exhaust from attached garages

The [Wisconsin Department of Health Services (DHS)](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) adds that electric and solar-powered appliances do not produce CO.

## How serious is the risk?

According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html), more than 400 Americans die each year from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires. More than 100,000 visit an emergency department and more than 14,000 are hospitalized. In Wisconsin, [DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) says about 500 people go to the emergency room each year because of CO.

The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) lists the most common symptoms:

- headache
- dizziness
- weakness
- upset stomach and vomiting
- chest pain
- confusion

These are often described as "flu-like." Infants, older adults and people with chronic heart disease, anemia or breathing problems are more likely to get sick. People who are asleep or intoxicated can die before they notice any symptoms. That is the main reason alarms matter.

## Why winter is higher risk

A CDC analysis published in [Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/media/pressrel/fs050120.htm) found that CO exposures happen more often in fall and winter, when people use gas furnaces, heaters and generators. The highest counts came in December and January. That data is from 2001 to 2003, so the exact numbers are dated, but the seasonal pattern is the useful takeaway for a Wisconsin household going into a long heating season.

## Where alarms should go

Wisconsin is specific here. [DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) says state law requires every home and multi-family building to have a CO detector on each floor, including the basement. Detectors are not required in attics or storage areas.

Federal guidance points the same way. The [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center) recommends battery-operated CO alarms, or alarms with battery backup, "on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas." The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) also recommends detectors near every sleeping area.

Put simply: at least one alarm on every floor, including the basement, plus one near the bedrooms so it can wake people up. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for exact mounting height and spacing. Those instructions vary by model, and this post doesn't cover them.

## Practical steps before and during furnace season

1. **Count your alarms.** Walk each floor, basement included, and confirm there's a working CO alarm on each one and near the bedrooms ([Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm), [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center)).
2. **Check the batteries when the clocks change.** The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) and [DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) both suggest the spring and fall time changes as a reminder. If you have plug-in or hardwired units, DHS says to make sure they have battery backup for power outages.
3. **Check the age.** Replace detectors every five years or as the manufacturer says ([Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm), [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html)). DHS notes new detectors usually cost $20 to $50 at hardware stores.
4. **Consider a digital readout.** The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) says these models can show the highest CO level recorded in your home, not just sound an alarm.
5. **Get fuel-burning equipment serviced every year.** The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) recommends having a qualified technician service your heating system, water heater, and any other gas, oil or coal appliances every year. It also recommends checking or cleaning the chimney every year, since debris can block it and cause CO to build up indoors.
6. **Know the never-do list.** [DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) says:
   - Never run a gas or propane heater, or a gas or charcoal grill, inside your home or garage.
   - Never run a car in an enclosed garage, even with a door open.
   - Never run a generator within 20 feet of the house.

## If the alarm goes off

[Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/air/co.htm) advice is straightforward:

- Get fresh air right away.
- Move everyone outside, then call the fire department.
- Don't go back in until the fire department has inspected the home and declared it safe.

Take symptoms like headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, shortness of breath or chest pain seriously.

## Where ductwork fits in

To be clear about what does what: duct cleaning is not a furnace safety inspection, and it is not a substitute for CO alarms. Servicing fuel-burning appliances is a job for a qualified technician, and CDC guidance is to have that done every year. Ductwork affects how air moves through your home and how clean it is. A company like [Ducts On Call](https://ductsoncall.com) can inspect and clean ducts and dryer vents. If you're booking that kind of service this fall, add an alarm check and an annual furnace service to the same list.

Furnace season is also when Wisconsin health officials suggest testing for radon. See our earlier post on [winter radon testing](/2026/10/22/radon-testing-wisconsin-winter/).

## Bottom line

Carbon monoxide can't be seen or smelled, and the risk rises in fall and winter. Wisconsin law requires a CO detector on every floor, including the basement, and federal guidance adds alarms near sleeping areas. Check the batteries when the clocks change, replace units about every five years, have fuel-burning appliances serviced every year, and if an alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call the fire department.
