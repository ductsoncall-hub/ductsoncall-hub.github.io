Once the furnace is running every day, many Wisconsin homes swing between two complaints. Some feel dry, with static shocks and dry skin. Others get water or frost on the inside of the windows on cold mornings. Both come down to relative humidity, and the right target in January isn't the same as in July.

## The general rule: below 60%, ideally 30–50%

The [EPA's guide to mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60%, and ideally between 30% and 50%. It also points out that "condensation can be a sign of high humidity." State guidance can be stricter. The [Wisconsin Department of Health Services (DHS)](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/mold/indoor.htm) advises keeping indoor humidity below 50% and says, "If indoor moisture is controlled, mold will not grow."

The reason is simple. Mold needs moisture, and both agencies treat moisture control as the main way to prevent it. For more on what happens once materials get wet, see our post on [mold after water damage](/2026/10/22/mold-after-water-damage-24-48-hours/).

## Why the winter target is lower

Warm indoor air can hold more water vapor than cold air. As the [EPA's mold course](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2) puts it, "When warm, humid air contacts a cold surface, condensation may form." Window glass, exterior wall corners and cold-water pipes are the usual spots. The colder it is outside, the colder those surfaces get, and the less indoor humidity it takes to wet them.

That's why cold-climate agencies recommend lower winter targets. The [Minnesota Department of Health](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/air/mold/index.html), writing for a climate a lot like ours, suggests keeping relative humidity between 20% and 40% in winter and under 60% the rest of the year, "to keep indoor surfaces as dry as possible."

Minnesota's [Center for Energy and Environment (CEE)](https://www.mncee.org/managing-humidity-levels), a nonprofit energy-efficiency organization, publishes a sliding scale tied to outdoor temperature:

| Outdoor temperature (°F) | Keep indoor RH below |
|---|---|
| 20 to 40 | 40% |
| 10 to 20 | 35% |
| 0 to 10 | 30% |
| −10 to 0 | 25% |
| −20 to −10 | 20% |

CEE notes that in extreme cold, even 40% can cause problem condensation on windows and walls. These numbers are a starting point, not a hard rule. Homes with well-insulated, newer windows can usually handle a bit more humidity than drafty homes with single-pane glass. Your windows are the best indicator.

## Do you need a humidifier at all?

Maybe not. [Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/mold/indoor.htm) says that "typically, it is not necessary to use humidifiers in homes," because everyday activities like cooking, showering and breathing add enough moisture. If your home still feels uncomfortably dry, [CEE](https://www.mncee.org/managing-humidity-levels) suggests running a humidifier for short periods at a low setting, around 30% to 40%. It also says that if moisture keeps building up on windows or walls, you should turn the humidifier off and lower the setting before turning it back on.

The [EPA's humidifier guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers) is firm on the upper limit: don't humidify above 50%, because higher levels "may encourage the growth of biological organisms in the home." If water condenses on windows, walls or pictures, EPA says to move the humidifier, lower its setting, or use it less.

### Keep the humidifier itself clean

A humidifier with standing water can become a source of contamination. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/use-and-care-home-humidifiers) recommends:

- For portable units, empty the tank, wipe the surfaces dry and refill daily. Unplug the unit first.
- Clean portable units every third day and remove any scale or film. If the manufacturer doesn't recommend a cleaner, EPA suggests a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, then a thorough rinse.
- Ultrasonic and impeller ("cool mist") types generally spread the most minerals and microorganisms. Minerals from tap water can show up as "white dust," and EPA suggests distilled water to cut down on mineral buildup.
- For central (furnace-mounted) humidifiers, follow the manufacturer's cleaning directions, and don't let water stand in the tank for long periods.

## Practical steps for this heating season

1. **Measure, don't guess.** According to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), a humidity meter is a small, inexpensive ($10–$50) tool sold at many hardware stores. Put one in your main living area and check it on the coldest mornings.
2. **Use your windows as a gauge.** Regular water or frost on the inside of the glass means your humidity is too high for the current outdoor temperature. Lower it until the windows stay mostly clear.
3. **Run exhaust fans.** [Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/mold/indoor.htm) recommends running bathroom fans during showers and for 20 minutes afterward. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) adds using exhaust fans while cooking or running the dishwasher.
4. **Vent moisture outdoors.** The [Minnesota Department of Health](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/air/mold/index.html) recommends making sure exhaust fans are ducted to the outside, not into the attic. It also says to vent appliances like clothes dryers, water heaters, furnaces and gas fireplaces outside.
5. **Warm up cold spots.** [MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/air/mold/index.html) and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) both suggest insulating cold pipes and improving warmth and air movement in colder parts of the home.
6. **Act fast on condensation.** If moisture collects on windows, walls or pipes, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says to dry the surface quickly and reduce the moisture source.

## Where HVAC fits in

Your heating system affects winter moisture in a few ways. A furnace-mounted humidifier can over-humidify if its setting is never adjusted. A clothes dryer that isn't vented outdoors adds moisture to the house, which is why the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) recommends venting dryers outside. If you're not sure your dryer vent or whole-house humidifier is set up and working properly, a [duct and indoor air professional](https://ductsoncall.com) can take a look. A humidity meter and a few cold-morning window checks will tell you a lot before you call anyone, though.

## Bottom line

Year-round, the EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60%, ideally 30% to 50%, and Wisconsin DHS advises staying under 50%. In a Wisconsin winter, the practical target is lower, often 30% to 40% or less on the coldest days, because cold windows and walls condense moisture at lower humidity. Measure with an inexpensive meter, let your windows guide you, run exhaust fans, and if you use a humidifier, keep the setting low and the unit clean.
