Dust mites are a common indoor allergen, and beds are one of their main habitats. Advice online often says to "buy allergy covers" and leave it at that. The research is more nuanced: some steps clearly lower mite allergen levels, but whether they improve symptoms depends on doing several things together. And it only matters if you're actually allergic to mites.

## What dust mites are and where they live

The [National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS)](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/allergens/dustmites) describes dust mites as "microscopic, insect-like pests that commonly live in house dust." They feed on flakes of dead skin shed by people and pets. They live in mattresses, bedding, upholstered furniture, carpets and curtains.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/asthma/asthma-triggers-gain-control) says "every home has dust mites." It explains that their body parts and droppings can trigger asthma in people allergic to them. The goal isn't a mite-free home. It's lower exposure for the people who react.

## What the evidence says about mite control

This is where honest summaries differ from marketing.

- **Single measures often don't help symptoms.** A 2008 [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD001187_does-controlling-exposure-house-dust-mites-improve-asthma) pooled 55 trials with 3,121 people with asthma. It found no effect of chemical or physical mite-control methods, including mattress covers, on asthma symptoms, medication use or peak flow. Many trials were of poor quality, and the authors concluded these methods "cannot be recommended."
- **Allergy symptoms in the nose: limited evidence.** A [Cochrane review on allergic rhinitis](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD001563_house-dust-mite-avoidance-measures-perennial-allergic-rhinitis) found that mite-killing chemicals and extensive bedroom-wide control programs might reduce symptoms for some people, "but the evidence is not strong." It said impermeable bedding used on its own "is unlikely to offer clinical benefit."
- **Current U.S. guidance: combine measures.** The NIH's [2020 asthma guideline update](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Asthma%20Clinicians%20Guide%20508_02-03-21.pdf) says the reviewed studies "provided little evidence" that allergen mitigation improves asthma outcomes overall. But for people with asthma who are allergic to dust mites, it conditionally recommends impermeable pillow and mattress covers "only as part of a multicomponent allergen mitigation intervention, not as a single-component intervention." For people with asthma who aren't sensitized to indoor allergens, it conditionally recommends against these home interventions as routine care.

The practical reading: if you or your child has a confirmed or strongly suspected mite allergy, a bundle of bedroom steps is reasonable and low-risk. If nobody in the house is mite-allergic, there's little reason to go to great lengths. A clinician or allergist can help sort out which applies to you.

## Practical steps for the bedroom

These steps come from [NIEHS](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/allergens/dustmites) and [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/asthma/asthma-triggers-gain-control). Given the evidence above, plan to do several of them together rather than relying on any one.

1. **Encase the mattress and pillows.** Use dust-proof or "allergen impermeable" zippered covers. NIEHS and EPA both recommend this. The NIH guideline says to pair it with other measures.
2. **Wash bedding weekly in hot water.** NIEHS specifies 130°F to 140°F to kill dust mites. EPA adds to dry everything completely. NIEHS says non-washable bedding can be frozen overnight.
3. **Keep humidity at or below 50%.** NIEHS suggests a dehumidifier or air conditioner to do this. In a Wisconsin winter, the safe target can be even lower. See our post on [winter indoor humidity](/2026/10/23/indoor-humidity-wisconsin-winter/) for how to check it without causing window condensation.
4. **Swap hard-to-wash items.** NIEHS suggests:
   - replacing wool or feather bedding with synthetic materials
   - choosing washable stuffed animals
   - where possible, swapping bedroom wall-to-wall carpet for bare floors, and removing fabric curtains and upholstered furniture
5. **Dust damp, not dry.** NIEHS says to use a damp mop or rag and "never use a dry cloth, as it stirs up allergens." EPA likewise recommends dusting often with a damp cloth.
6. **Vacuum with a HEPA filter or double-layer bag.** EPA recommends weekly vacuuming of carpets and furniture. NIEHS suggests a double-layered microfilter bag or a HEPA filter in the vacuum.
7. **Let the dust settle.** NIEHS advises wearing a mask while vacuuming and staying out of the room for 20 minutes afterward. EPA says people with asthma or allergies should leave the area being vacuumed.

## Where HVAC fits in

Dust mites live mainly in mattresses, bedding, upholstery and carpets ([NIEHS](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/allergens/dustmites)). Bedroom steps do most of the work, and no furnace filter or duct service replaces them. A well-fitted filter is still part of a reasonable whole-home approach to dust. The EPA's [duct cleaning guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) recommends using the highest-efficiency filter your system's manufacturer recommends and changing it regularly. It also notes that a light amount of household dust in ducts is normal. It suggests cleaning only when there's a specific reason, such as heavy debris or dust actually blowing out of the registers. If that's what you're seeing, a [duct professional](https://ductsoncall.com) can inspect the system and show you what's there.

## Bottom line

Dust mites are in every home. They only matter if someone in the house is allergic. For people who are, the evidence says single fixes like mattress covers alone usually don't improve symptoms. NIH guidance supports a bundle instead:

- encase mattresses and pillows
- wash bedding weekly in hot water
- keep humidity at or below 50%
- dust with a damp cloth
- vacuum with a HEPA filter

Talk with a clinician if allergy or asthma symptoms persist.
