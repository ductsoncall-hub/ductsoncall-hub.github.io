Most people who call about "bad air" are guessing. A smell, a headache in one room, dust on the registers. A monitor replaces the guess with a number, and the number usually points somewhere specific: a humidity problem, a combustion source, a filtration gap, or nothing at all. This guide covers which readings are worth paying for and which monitors hold up in independent testing.

## The readings that matter

**PM2.5.** Fine particles under 2.5 microns are the pollutant with the strongest health evidence indoors and out; our [PM2.5 indoors](/2026/10/22/pm25-indoors-what-the-science-says/) article covers the science. A PM2.5 sensor tells you whether cooking, a wood stove, outdoor smoke or a dusty duct system is loading your air, and whether your filtration is keeping up.

**Humidity.** The cheapest and most actionable reading. The EPA's mold-prevention target is below 60% relative humidity, ideally 30% to 50% ([EPA Mold Course](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2)). A logging monitor shows whether your [dehumidifier](/2026/10/23/best-dehumidifier-for-wisconsin-basement/) actually holds that range overnight.

**CO2.** Not toxic at household levels, but a direct proxy for how much fresh air a room is getting. Rising CO2 in a bedroom overnight means the house is tight and stale, which also means every other pollutant builds up. Look for a true NDIR CO2 sensor; some cheap units estimate CO2 from VOC readings instead, which is not the same measurement ([Verdict Reviews](https://verdict-reviews.com/product/airgradient-one/)).

**Radon.** The Wisconsin Department of Health Services says about 1 in 10 Wisconsin homes has elevated radon, and you cannot tell which by looking ([Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/news/releases/010625.htm)). The only way to know a home's level is to measure it. A continuous radon reading is unique to a few monitors and is the main reason to pay more; see our [radon testing in winter](/2026/10/22/radon-testing-wisconsin-winter/) article for why the heating season is the right time.

**VOCs and NOx.** Useful as trend indicators, but understand the limit: the common Sensirion SGP41 sensor reports relative changes in VOC and NOx levels, not absolute concentrations ([Wirecutter](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/best-home-air-quality-monitor/)). A spike after you open a can of paint is real information; the specific number is not a lab result.

## How to use the data

1. **Put the monitor where you sleep first.** Bedrooms are where you spend eight hours with the door closed. Overnight CO2 and humidity trends there are the most useful week of data you will collect.
2. **Move it around.** A week in the basement, a week by the kitchen, a week in the room that "smells." Patterns, not single readings, are the point.
3. **Watch what happens when the furnace runs.** A PM2.5 spike each time the blower starts suggests the duct system or filter is a source. That is worth an inspection before it is worth a purifier.
4. **Do not treat it as a mold test.** No consumer monitor detects mold or mycotoxins. It detects the humidity that lets mold grow, which is more useful for prevention. For what is and is not known about mycotoxin exposure, see [our review of the evidence](/2026/10/22/mycotoxins-what-is-known/).
