That "new" smell from a flat-pack dresser, new cabinets or fresh laminate flooring is partly a mix of chemicals coming off the materials. Formaldehyde is one of the best known. People usually want to know two things: is it harmful, and how long will it last? Public health agencies give fairly clear answers to both, along with some advice that's less obvious, like why an air purifier isn't the fix.

## Where formaldehyde comes from at home

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/facts-about-formaldehyde) lists formaldehyde in:

- resins used to make composite wood like hardwood plywood, particleboard and medium-density fiberboard (MDF)
- building materials and insulation
- glues, permanent-press fabrics, paints, lacquers and finishes

It's also a combustion byproduct, so it comes from unvented fuel-burning appliances like gas stoves and kerosene heaters, and from cigarette smoke.

The [Wisconsin Department of Health Services](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/chemical/formaldehyde.htm) makes a useful distinction. Wood products made with urea-formaldehyde (UF) resins give off more formaldehyde than those made with phenol-formaldehyde (PF) resins. UF resins are used in particleboard, hardwood plywood paneling and MDF, which are the materials inside a lot of furniture and cabinetry.

The [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) (part of the CDC) says levels tend to be higher in three kinds of homes:

- homes with smokers, where tobacco smoke may be the largest source
- homes with new products or new construction
- homes built after 1990, which are better insulated so less air moves in and out, letting formaldehyde linger

That last point matters in a Wisconsin winter, when houses are closed up for months.

## Is it harmful?

At the levels typical in most homes, the agencies are reassuring. The [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) says, "Most people don't have any health problems from small amounts of formaldehyde in their homes." As levels rise, some people get breathing problems or irritation of the eyes, nose, throat or skin. Children, older adults and people with asthma or other breathing problems are more likely to notice symptoms.

[Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/chemical/formaldehyde.htm) says that above 0.1 parts per million (ppm), formaldehyde may irritate the eyes, throat and nose. It adds that inhaling formaldehyde may trigger asthma symptoms. On long-term risk, ATSDR's [ToxFAQs](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts111.pdf) notes that the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services determined in 2011 that formaldehyde is a known human carcinogen. That conclusion was based on studies of breathing it. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/facts-about-formaldehyde) puts it as: "High levels of exposure may cause some types of cancers."

One thing people are often surprised by: there's no official safe-or-unsafe line for homes. ATSDR says, "there are no standards for acceptable levels of formaldehyde in your home."

## So how long does off-gassing last?

The short answer: emissions are highest when a product is new, then drop over time.

- The [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) says, "The levels reduce over time. Most formaldehyde is released by 2 years," citing research by Park and Ikeda (2006).
- [Wisconsin DHS](https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/chemical/formaldehyde.htm) says formaldehyde from wood products or fabrics decreases as products age.
- When products are new, high temperature and humidity may increase the release.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/protect-against-exposures-formaldehyde) agrees that heat speeds up formaldehyde release, and that release "may also depend somewhat on the humidity level."

In practice, the first weeks and months after a big purchase or remodel are when ventilation matters most. Two years is a general guide from the research, not a deadline that applies to every product.

## Buying lower-emitting products

Since 2019, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/formaldehyde-emission-standards-composite-wood-products) has required hardwood plywood, MDF and particleboard sold in the U.S., including finished goods containing them, to be certified and labeled TSCA Title VI compliant. This doesn't mean "zero formaldehyde." It means the product meets federal emission limits. Look for that label on furniture and cabinet boxes.

The [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) also suggests choosing:

- furniture, cabinetry or flooring made without UF glues
- pressed-wood products that meet "ultra-low emitting formaldehyde" (ULEF) or "no added formaldehyde" (NAF) requirements
- products labeled "No VOC/Low VOC"

## Practical steps after bringing home something new

These steps come from [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/protect-against-exposures-formaldehyde) unless noted.

1. **Air it out first if you can.** ATSDR suggests letting new products release formaldehyde in a garage or on a patio before using them inside, ideally until you can no longer smell a chemical odor.
2. **Ventilate, especially early on.** EPA says increasing ventilation "particularly after bringing new sources of formaldehyde into the home" helps reduce levels. ATSDR suggests opening windows for a few minutes every few days. The exception is if outdoor pollution or pollen triggers your asthma, or if safety is a concern.
3. **Run exhaust fans** as much as possible ([ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html)).
4. **Keep the house cooler and drier.** ATSDR recommends the lowest comfortable temperature and humidity. EPA notes air conditioning and dehumidifiers can help, and says to drain and clean dehumidifier trays often. In winter, see our post on [indoor humidity targets](/2026/10/23/indoor-humidity-wisconsin-winter/).
5. **Wash permanent-press clothing and curtains** before using them.
6. **Make the home smoke-free.**
7. **Don't count on an air purifier or "bake-out."** According to [ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html), "Air filters generally don't help lower levels of formaldehyde in your home." Overheating the house to "bake out" formaldehyde "doesn't work and may even raise formaldehyde levels."

## Should you test?

[ATSDR](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_atsdr_cdc_gov/formaldehyde/home/index.html) says testing usually isn't needed unless you still smell strong chemical odors, or have breathing problems or irritation only when you're at home. If you test, it recommends a qualified professional. It notes that professional tests can be expensive and don't show which product is the main source, and that home kit results can vary with where and how long you sample. If you have symptoms, talk with a health care provider.

## Where ventilation fits in

Since filters generally don't remove formaldehyde, fresh air and working exhaust fans do most of the work. If your bathroom or kitchen fans seem weak, or you're not sure where they vent, a [ventilation and duct professional](https://ductsoncall.com) can check that they move air outdoors the way they should.

## Bottom line

Formaldehyde from new furniture, cabinets and flooring is highest at first and declines over time. ATSDR says most is released within about two years. Most people have no health problems from the small amounts typical in homes, but sensitive people may notice irritation. Pick TSCA Title VI, ULEF or NAF products, air new items out, ventilate and run exhaust fans, and keep the house cool and dry. Don't rely on air filters or bake-outs.
