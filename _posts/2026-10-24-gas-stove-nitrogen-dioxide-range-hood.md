Gas stoves became a political argument a few years ago, and much of what's online leans hard one way or the other. The underlying science is more nuanced. Burning gas indoors does release pollutants, and venting them outside clearly helps. How much gas cooking contributes to childhood asthma is still debated. This post covers what's well established, what isn't, and what you can do in your own kitchen.

## What a gas flame adds to indoor air

The pollutant most associated with gas cooking is nitrogen dioxide (NO2). The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/nitrogen-dioxides-impact-indoor-air-quality) lists unvented combustion appliances, "e.g. gas stoves," among the main indoor sources. Others include vented appliances with defective installations, kerosene heaters and tobacco smoke.

EPA also notes that the average NO2 level in homes without combustion appliances is about half the outdoor level. In homes with gas stoves, kerosene heaters or unvented gas space heaters, "indoor levels often exceed outdoor levels."

A 2023 review in the [American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10112436/) adds that NO2 is the main health-relevant pollutant emitted by gas cooking but not electric cooking. Incomplete gas combustion can also produce formaldehyde, carbon monoxide and ultrafine particles. The same review notes that peak indoor NO2 from gas cooking often exceeds the EPA's 1-hour outdoor standard of 100 ppb.

## What NO2 can do

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/nitrogen-dioxides-impact-indoor-air-quality) describes NO2 as mainly an irritant of the eyes, nose, throat and respiratory tract. It says low-level exposure may cause:

- increased bronchial reactivity in some people with asthma
- decreased lung function in people with COPD
- increased risk of respiratory infections, especially in young children

Those are EPA's general statements about NO2. They aren't a diagnosis for any particular household. If you have symptoms, talk with a clinician.

## The gas stove and asthma debate

This is where the evidence is genuinely mixed, and it's worth seeing both sides.

**Studies that found an association.** A 2013 meta-analysis in the [International Journal of Epidemiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23962958/) pooled 41 studies. It found that gas cooking was associated with higher odds of asthma in children (odds ratio 1.32), and indoor NO2 was associated with current wheeze. The authors concluded that gas cooking increases the risk of asthma in children.

**Studies that didn't.** A global analysis of more than 500,000 children in 47 countries, published in [Lancet Respiratory Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24429203/) in 2013, found no evidence of an association between gas cooking and asthma symptoms or diagnosis. It did link cooking over an open fire to more wheeze.

**Disputes about study quality.** A 2023 systematic review in [Global Epidemiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37638371/) concluded that the research is limited by "high heterogeneity and low study quality." It said the evidence isn't sufficient to show a causal relationship, and cautioned against over-interpreting meta-analysis estimates. Readers should know that review was [funded by the American Gas Association](https://www.aga.org/news/news-releases/new-study-finds-research-on-natural-gas-cooking-and-asthma-fails-to-demonstrate-causal-relationship/), which said it wasn't involved in drafting the paper.

A fair summary: many studies link gas cooking with childhood asthma or wheeze, some large studies don't, and researchers disagree about how strong and causal the link is. You don't need to settle that debate to act sensibly. The steps that cut NO2 exposure are simple and inexpensive.

## Range hoods: what the measurements show

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/nitrogen-dioxides-impact-indoor-air-quality) says venting NO2 sources outdoors, and making sure combustion appliances are correctly installed, used and maintained, are the most effective ways to reduce exposure. Its specific advice is to "install and use an exhaust fan vented to outdoors over gas stoves."

Field measurements back this up:

- **California homes study.** A study of homes with gas stoves in California's San Joaquin Valley, led by researchers at [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://energyanalysis.lbl.gov/publications/range-hood-use-and-effectiveness), tracked thousands of cooking events. Integrated NO2 levels were about 45% higher during cooking without the range hood than with it. Pollution was lowest when the hood ran for more than half the cooking time.
- **Bay Area pilot trial.** A small 14-home trial in the [Journal of Exposure Science & Environmental Epidemiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39521897/) showed families a short video on switching to back burners and using the range hood. Afterward, back-burner use rose substantially and hood use rose slightly. Measured NO2 dropped even though cooking time didn't change. No changes in children's respiratory measures were seen in that short pilot.

## Practical steps for a gas kitchen

1. **Turn the hood on every time you cook,** and leave it running for the whole session. The LBL data showed the best results when the hood ran for more than half the cooking time.
2. **Use the back burners when you can.** Switching to back burners was part of the intervention that lowered NO2 in the [pilot trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39521897/).
3. **Make sure the hood vents outdoors.** EPA's advice is specifically for an exhaust fan "vented to outdoors." Some hoods and over-the-range microwaves just recirculate air through a filter. If yours doesn't connect to a duct leading outside, it isn't doing what EPA describes.
4. **Keep gas appliances adjusted and maintained** ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/nitrogen-dioxides-impact-indoor-air-quality)). EPA also recommends an annual professional inspection and tune-up of furnaces, flues and chimneys.
5. **Never heat your house with a gas oven** ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html)), and keep working CO alarms. See our post on [where carbon monoxide alarms belong](/2026/10/23/carbon-monoxide-alarms-wisconsin-furnace-season/).

## Bottom line

Gas stoves release nitrogen dioxide, and EPA says indoor levels in homes with gas stoves often exceed outdoor levels. Research linking gas cooking to childhood asthma is real but disputed, partly over study quality and partly over who funded which review. Either way, the practical answer is the same: use a range hood that vents outdoors every time you cook, favor the back burners, and keep combustion appliances maintained.
