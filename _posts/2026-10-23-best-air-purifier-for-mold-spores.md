Mold spores are particles, and a good portable air cleaner removes particles. That is the whole case for using one in a musty room, and it is a real one. But it comes with a limit the EPA states plainly: air cleaners "do not solve mold problems or address the cause of mold and musty odors," which is always a water or moisture problem ([EPA Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)). Fix the moisture first, then use a purifier to clean up what stays airborne. Here is how to choose one.

## What actually matters

**HEPA filtration.** Mold spores typically range from about 1 to 40 microns, well above the smallest particles HEPA media is rated for, which is why HEPA-rated units are described as capturing up to 99.97% of airborne particles at 0.3 microns ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/air-purifier/reviews/best/mold)). Any true HEPA unit will catch spores; the differences between models are about how much air they move and how loud they are doing it.

**CADR sized to the room.** The EPA's rule is simple: pick a unit whose clean air delivery rate is large enough for the room, and use the tobacco-smoke CADR because it represents the smallest particles. Its sizing chart calls for roughly 65 cfm per 100 square feet with 8-foot ceilings, so a 400 square foot room needs a CADR of about 260 cfm, and open floor plans or tall ceilings need more ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)).

**No ozone.** The EPA advises avoiding any air cleaner that intentionally produces ozone, and warns that ionizers, electrostatic precipitators, plasma units and poorly shielded UV lamps may emit some. No federal agency has approved ozone generators for occupied spaces ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)). If a unit has an ionizer, make sure it can be switched off.

**Carbon for the smell.** A HEPA filter removes spores, not the musty odor, which is a gas. A unit with a meaningful activated carbon stage can help with odors, though the EPA notes it will still not resolve the underlying mold problem.

## How to use it in a musty basement

- **Run it continuously on a medium speed** rather than occasionally on high. The EPA notes that longer run times and higher fan speeds increase the amount of air filtered ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)).
- **Pair it with a dehumidifier.** The EPA's mold guidance is to keep indoor relative humidity below 60%, ideally between 30% and 50% ([EPA Mold Course](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2)). A purifier cannot do that; see our [basement dehumidifier guide](/2026/10/23/best-dehumidifier-for-wisconsin-basement/).
- **Change filters on schedule.** A loaded HEPA filter moves less air.
- **Check the ducts.** If a musty smell arrives when the furnace runs, the source may be inside the return ducts or on the coil, where a room purifier cannot reach. That is an inspection job, not a purchase.

## What a purifier will not fix

If there is visible mold on a wall, under a carpet or inside ductwork, no purifier changes that. The EPA's position is that cleaning up the mold and removing the moisture source is the fix; air cleaning is a supplement ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home)).
