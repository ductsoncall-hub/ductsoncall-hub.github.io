A dehumidifier is the one appliance that directly attacks the condition mold needs: moisture in the air. The EPA's target is unambiguous. Keep indoor relative humidity below 60%, and ideally between 30% and 50% ([EPA Mold Course](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2)). In a Wisconsin basement from May through September, hitting that number without a dehumidifier is nearly impossible, because cool concrete walls and floors keep the air near its dew point all summer.

This guide covers how to size one, what the ratings mean in a cold basement, and which models independent testers rate highest.

## First, rule out a water problem

Consumer Reports makes a point that matches what we see in the field: dehumidifiers are meant to make damp spaces comfortable, not to dry puddles or carpet that squishes underfoot. If water is getting in, look for the source, clean the gutters, extend the downspouts away from the foundation, and only then expect a dehumidifier to hold the room between 30% and 50% ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/dehumidifiers/best-dehumidifiers-for-basements-crawl-spaces-damp-areas-a1075055307/)).

## Sizing: buy bigger than you think

Consumer Reports' advice is to buy a larger capacity than you technically need; it dries the room faster and you empty the tank less often. Their categories are large (40 pints or more per day) for big or genuinely damp spaces, medium (30 to 40 pints) for midsize somewhat-damp rooms, and small (under 30) for small damp spaces ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/dehumidifiers/best-dehumidifiers-for-basements-crawl-spaces-damp-areas-a1075055307/)). For a typical full Wisconsin basement, that means a 50-pint unit.

**About the pint rating.** In 2019 the U.S. Department of Energy moved the capacity test from 80°F to 65°F, so the same hardware now measures roughly 25 to 30% lower, and a unit once labeled 70 pints is sold as about 50 pints today ([Dehumidifier Tips](https://dehumidifiertips.com/dehumidifier-pint-ratings-explained/)). The cooler test condition is close to a real basement. Consumer Reports runs its own water-removal test in a chamber at the same 65°F and 60% RH ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/dehumidifiers/best-dehumidifiers-for-basements-crawl-spaces-damp-areas-a1075055307/)). Below about 60°F, compressor units lose efficiency and may ice up, so frost control matters in a cold basement.

## Three features worth paying for

1. **A built-in pump.** Gravity drain only works if a floor drain or sump sits below the unit. A pump lets you run the hose up to a utility sink. Both Energy Star-listed Midea and Frigidaire pump models below lift water roughly 14 to 16 feet ([OwnerSpec](https://ownerspec.com/plumbing/reviews/best-dehumidifier-for-basement/)).
2. **Auto-restart and a continuous mode.** After a power blip, the unit should resume on its own.
3. **Frost control.** Prevents ice on the coils, which stops dehumidifying ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/dehumidifiers/)).

## Set it and verify it

Set the humidistat to 50% or lower and leave it in continuous drain if you can. Then measure independently: built-in humidistats can read several points off, and Consumer Reports scores humidistat accuracy for exactly that reason ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/appliances/dehumidifiers/best-dehumidifiers-for-basements-crawl-spaces-damp-areas-a1075055307/)). A $15 hygrometer on the opposite side of the room, or an [air quality monitor](/2026/10/23/best-indoor-air-quality-monitor/) that logs humidity, tells you whether the unit is actually holding the EPA range.
