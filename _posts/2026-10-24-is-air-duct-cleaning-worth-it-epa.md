"Should I get my ducts cleaned?" is one of the most common indoor air questions, and you'll find plenty of confident answers online in both directions. A widely cited neutral source is the U.S. EPA's consumer guide, [Should You Have the Air Ducts in Your Home Cleaned?](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned). This post summarizes what it says. This blog is [sponsored by a duct cleaning company](/about/), so we think it's especially important to be straight about where the evidence is strong and where it isn't.

One caveat up front: the EPA guide was first published in 1997 (EPA 402-K-97-002). It's still posted as EPA's consumer guidance on the topic, but much of the research it refers to is old. The agency itself says knowledge about duct cleaning "is in its early stages."

## What the evidence does and doesn't show

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) is direct about the limits:

- "Duct cleaning has never been shown to actually prevent health problems."
- Studies don't conclusively show that dust levels in homes rise because of dirty ducts, or fall after cleaning. EPA explains that much of the dirt in ducts sticks to duct surfaces and doesn't necessarily reach the living space.
- Everyday sources like outdoor air, cooking, cleaning, smoking, or "just moving around" can expose you to more contaminants than dirty ducts do.
- There's no evidence that a light amount of household dust in ducts poses a health risk. EPA calls a light amount of dust in ducts "normal."

On whether duct cleaning prevents health problems in general, EPA's answer is: "no one knows." It does note that badly contaminated ducts can spread contaminants through a home, and "in these cases, duct cleaning may make sense."

## When EPA says to consider cleaning

The [EPA guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) lists three situations:

1. **Substantial visible mold growth** inside hard-surface (sheet metal) ducts or on other parts of the heating and cooling system.
2. **Vermin** in the ducts, such as rodents or insects.
3. **Ducts clogged with excessive dust and debris**, or particles actually being released into the home from supply registers.

On mold, EPA adds a few important cautions:

- Something that looks like mold can only be confirmed by an expert and may need lab analysis.
- Wet or moldy insulation inside ducts can't be cleaned effectively and should be removed and replaced.
- If the cause of the moisture isn't fixed, the mold will come back.

That last point applies to all three situations. EPA says the underlying cause must be corrected before any cleaning, or the problem will likely recur. For background on why moisture is the key, see our post on [mold after water damage](/2026/10/22/mold-after-water-damage-24-48-hours/).

EPA also says routine cleaning isn't recommended, only cleaning as needed. Still, if you choose to clean simply because it seems sensible, "no evidence suggests that such cleaning would be detrimental," as long as it's done properly.

## What about energy efficiency?

According to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned), some research suggests that cleaning cooling coils, fans and heat exchangers may improve system efficiency. But "little evidence exists" that cleaning only the ducts does. Sealing duct air leaks is a different service from coating the inside of ducts, and EPA notes that sealing leaks can help save energy.

## Biocides and sealants

Some providers offer to spray chemical biocides or apply sealants inside the ducts. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) says research hasn't demonstrated how effective these are in duct cleaning, or what health effects they might have. It also says:

- There are no EPA-registered biocides for use on fiberglass duct board or fiberglass-lined ducts.
- If these products are used at all, it should only be after the system has been properly cleaned.
- Don't allow them unless you fully understand the pros and cons.

## Practical steps if you're deciding

These steps follow the [EPA guide](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned):

1. **Look first.** Remove a register cover and use a flashlight. Dust on return grilles is normal and can just be vacuumed. If nobody at home has allergies or unexplained symptoms and you see no heavy dust, musty odor, or visible mold, cleaning is "probably unnecessary."
2. **Ask to see the problem.** Ask any provider to show you the contamination that justifies cleaning, including any mold they say they found.
3. **Get multiple quotes.** EPA suggests talking to at least three providers and getting written estimates.
4. **Watch for red flags.** EPA says not to hire anyone who makes sweeping health claims or recommends duct cleaning as routine maintenance. Be wary of anyone claiming EPA certification; EPA doesn't certify duct cleaners.
5. **Know what a good job includes.** The provider should:
   - clean the whole system, not just the ducts
   - check for asbestos-containing materials before starting
   - use vacuum equipment that exhausts outdoors, or HEPA equipment if it exhausts indoors
   - use soft brushes on fiberglass duct board or lined ducts
   - seal any access holes airtight
   - follow the National Air Duct Cleaners Association (NADCA) standards
6. **Inspect afterward.** EPA provides a post-cleaning checklist. All parts should be visibly clean, and light from a flashlight should pass through a clean cooling coil.

## Prevention matters more than cleaning

Whether or not you clean, EPA says the best way to prevent contamination is to keep water and dirt out of the system. Its recommendations:

- Use the highest-efficiency filter your system's manufacturer recommends, and change it regularly. See our post on [choosing a MERV rating](/2026/10/23/what-merv-filter-for-home-furnace/).
- Make sure air can't bypass the filter through gaps.
- Ask for the cooling coils and drain pans to be cleaned during routine service.
- Seal off registers during dusty renovation work.
- Repair leaks promptly, and make sure condensate pans drain properly.

EPA also recommends having fuel-burning furnaces, stoves and fireplaces inspected and serviced before each heating season to protect against carbon monoxide.

## Bottom line

EPA doesn't recommend routine duct cleaning. It says cleaning has never been shown to prevent health problems, and a light layer of dust in ducts is normal. Cleaning makes sense when there's substantial visible mold on hard surfaces, vermin, or heavy debris, and only after the cause is fixed. If you hire someone, ask to see the problem, avoid sweeping health claims, and check the work afterward.
